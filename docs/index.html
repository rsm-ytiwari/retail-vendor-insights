Project Overview

Every retail dataset has its heroes and villains — vendors that drive growth, and others that quietly trap cash. I built a vendor performance model using SQL, Python, and statistical analysis to uncover who’s driving sales, who’s protecting margins, and where money gets stuck.

What started as raw transactions turned into a clear story of leverage, pricing behavior, and capital flow — revealing exactly where the business gains power, and where it leaks it.

Key Insights

  • Vendor Concentration: Two-thirds of all purchases come from just ten vendors — efficient, but risky.
  • Margin–Sales Gap: Some brands show great margins but weak sales — unrealized potential.
  • Economies of Scale: Bulk orders cut unit costs by ~72%, confirming scale efficiency.
  • Inventory Drag: About $2.7M is locked in unsold stock, mostly from top vendors.
  • Profit Paradox: Smaller vendors average 41% margins, while top vendors run leaner at ~31% (p < 0.001).

Takeaway

The analysis revealed a two-speed ecosystem: large vendors drive revenue, niche ones drive profit. The opportunity lies in managing both — scaling where it compounds, protecting where it pays, and using data as the steering wheel.

Database Connection and Data Loading

The vendor data was stored in a PostgreSQL warehouse. I connected using SQLAlchemy with credentials managed through environment variables, keeping security and reproducibility in check.

From the table mart.vendor_sales_summary, I extracted vendor-level records including brand, product details, purchase costs, and selling prices. Each row represented a small financial transaction, but together they mapped the full vendor ecosystem — who sells what, at what cost, and how efficiently.

The dataset’s structure was well-suited for this analysis: clean relationships between sales, pricing, and inventory allowed for a direct evaluation of pricing efficiency, vendor competitiveness, and sales performance across brands.

Data Filtering Criteria

To ensure analytical accuracy, I applied a set of logical filters to remove invalid or incomplete records. The focus was on retaining only transactions that reflected genuine sales and profitability.

Filtering Conditions:

  • gross_profit > 0 — retained only profitable transactions.
  • profit_margin > 0 — excluded loss-making or zero-margin entries.
  • total_sales_quantity > 0 — ensured only records with actual sales activity remained.

After applying these conditions, the dataset reduced from 10,692 to 8,564 records — eliminating roughly 20% of inconsistent or non-representative entries. This refined dataset now provides a cleaner foundation for modeling vendor performance and profitability.

These brands make excellent margins but remain underexposed. * The upper-left cluster of the scatter plot captures these outliers clearly, separating them from the broader dataset. * The profit margin range (≈65–90%) shows strong pricing power, while sales volumes remain limited — often due to low visibility or shelf turnover. * The majority of products sit below the high-margin line, confirming that broad profitability remains moderate. * The clean separation between the clusters suggests structural inefficiency rather than random variance — a consistent group of brands is systematically underselling.

This isn’t a problem — it’s an opportunity hiding in plain sight. Brands with high margins and low sales are primed for bundling, cross-selling, or targeted campaigns. A small marketing push or strategic price adjustment could turn them from quiet performers into profit accelerators.

# to calculate the confidence interval
def confidence_interval(data, confidence=0.95):
    mean_val=np.mean(data)
    std_err=np.std(data,ddof=1)/np.sqrt(len(data)) #standard error
    t_critical=stats.t.ppf((1+confidence)/2, df=len(data)-1)
    margin_of_error= t_critical *std_err
    return mean_val, mean_val-margin_of_error, mean_val+margin_of_error
# Compute confidence intervals for top and low vendors

low_mean, low_lower, low_upper = confidence_interval(low_vendors)

Analysis 7 — Hypothesis Testing: Profit Margin Differences Between Vendor Groups

I wanted to confirm whether the profit gap I observed between top vendors and low vendors was just noise — or if it actually held up statistically.

Hypotheses
  • H₀: No significant difference exists in average profit margins between top- and low-performing vendors.
  • H₁: There is a significant difference in mean profit margins between the two groups.
Method

I ran a two-sample t-test assuming unequal variances.

  • Top vendors: sales above the 75th percentile
  • Low vendors: sales below the 25th percentile

This test checks whether their mean profit margins come from the same population.

Show code 
from scipy.stats import ttest_ind

# Define thresholds for top and low vendors (based on quartiles)
top_threshold = df["total_sales_dollars"].quantile(0.75)
low_threshold = df["total_sales_dollars"].quantile(0.25)

# Split vendors into top and low based on sales
top_vendors = df[df["total_sales_dollars"] >= top_threshold]["profit_margin"].dropna()
low_vendors = df[df["total_sales_dollars"] <= low_threshold]["profit_margin"].dropna()

# Perform Two-Sample T-Test
t_stat, p_value = ttest_ind(top_vendors, low_vendors, equal_var=False)

# Print results
print(f"T-Statistic: {t_stat:.4f}, P-Value: {p_value:.4f}")

if p_value < 0.05:
    print("Reject H₀: There is a significant difference in profit margins between top and low-performing vendors.")
else:
    print("Fail to Reject H₀: No significant difference in profit margins.")
